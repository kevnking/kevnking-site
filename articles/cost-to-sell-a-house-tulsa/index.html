What it costs to sell a house in Tulsa

The line-by-line math on a median-priced Tulsa house — including the three costs most online calculators leave out.

Selling a house in Tulsa costs most sellers between 7% and 10% of the sale price. On a $260,000 house — roughly the Tulsa median over the three months ending August 2026 — that is about $18,000 to $26,000 off the top. Commission is the largest single piece, usually 2.5% to 3% for the listing side plus whatever you agree to pay the buyer's agent. The rest is title and closing fees, Oklahoma's documentary stamp tax, prorated property taxes, prep work, and the credit you hand the buyer after inspection.

Every seller I meet has a number in their head, and it is almost always the Zillow estimate. Nobody walks in with the net. The gap between those two figures is what this article is about, and it is usually five figures.

Two caveats before the math. Nothing here is a quote — your contract, your title company and your closing date move these numbers. And I am not a tax advisor; the property tax and capital gains pieces are worth an hour with a CPA if your sale is large.

How much is real estate commission in Oklahoma?

Commission in Oklahoma is negotiable and always has been, and since the 2024 NAR settlement changed how buyer's agents get paid, the total is no longer a fixed 6%. What I see across Tulsa listings now is a listing side between 2.5% and 3%, with buyer's agent compensation negotiated separately and often handled as a concession inside the contract rather than a posted number on the MLS.

The practical effect for a seller is that the buyer-side piece is genuinely in play. You are not required to offer it. But understand what you are trading: most buyers in the $150,000 to $350,000 Tulsa bands are working with an agent and cannot pay that agent out of pocket on top of a down payment. Offering nothing narrows your buyer pool in exactly the price range where the pool matters most. In practice it usually comes back as a request for a concession anyway, which is the same money wearing a different hat.

Where commission is actually worth arguing about: a clean, updated house in a strong Tulsa submarket that will sell in two weeks is a different amount of work than a repair-heavy property that needs three price corrections. Price the service to the job. Any agent who will not have that conversation is telling you something.

What are seller closing costs in Oklahoma?

Oklahoma seller closing costs typically run 1% to 2% of the sale price, separate from commission. The largest pieces are title work and prorated property taxes, and the one that surprises people is the documentary stamp tax.

Documentary stamp tax. Oklahoma charges $0.75 per $500 of consideration, or any fraction of it, on deeds over $100 (68 O.S. § 3201). That is 0.15%, which is $390 on a $260,000 house. It is customarily the seller's line, and it is one of the cheaper transfer taxes in the country.

Title work. Oklahoma still runs on abstracts. An abstract of title is the bound history of everything ever recorded against the property, and in a sale it gets brought current by a licensed abstractor before the buyer's attorney or title company examines it. Updating the abstract has customarily been the seller's expense here, and the cost scales with how much has happened to the property — a straightforward update runs a few hundred dollars, while an abstract that has not been touched in forty years, or one covering a lot split, can run well past a thousand. Who pays for the owner's title policy is negotiable in Tulsa and varies deal to deal. Read your contract; do not assume the custom.

Prorated property taxes. Oklahoma property taxes are paid in arrears, so at closing you credit the buyer for the portion of the year you owned the house. Close in November and that credit is most of the annual bill. Close in February and it is small. Nothing is lost either way — you owed the tax — but it changes the wire amount meaningfully, and it is the line most sellers have not budgeted. You can pull your parcel's current figure from the Tulsa County Assessor before you list.

Closing fee and recording. The closing company's fee is commonly split between buyer and seller, putting the seller's share somewhere around $200 to $500 in the Tulsa market. Add a small amount for recording the release of your mortgage. If you have an HOA, expect a transfer or statement fee as well.

What do the online calculators leave out?

Three things, and together they are often larger than the closing costs the calculators do show.

Prep and repairs. The cost of getting a house listable is real and it is paid before any money comes back. Paint, carpet, a deep clean, hauling off what the garage has collected, a few thousand in deferred maintenance the buyer's inspector is going to find anyway. I have watched sellers spend $12,000 preparing a house and recover maybe $8,000 of it in price. The work that reliably pays is cosmetic and cheap: paint, light fixtures, landscaping, and getting the place genuinely empty. Major renovation before a sale almost never returns its cost.

The inspection credit. Almost every financed Tulsa sale has an inspection, and almost every inspection produces a request. Roof, HVAC age, foundation movement, electrical, plumbing under the slab. Budget 0% to 2% of the price for whatever comes back, because a deal that dies at inspection and goes back on the market costs you far more than the credit would have.

Carrying costs. Tulsa homes sold at a median 23 days on market over the three months ending August 2026, at about 97.9% of list price (Redfin). Add 30 to 45 days from contract to close on a financed deal and you are carrying the house roughly two more months — mortgage, taxes, insurance, utilities. That is real money, and it is the number that gets ugly when a house sits at the wrong price for ninety days.

Is it cheaper to sell a house as-is in Tulsa?

Cheaper on costs, usually lower on net. A direct as-is sale eliminates commission, prep, inspection credits and most of the carrying cost, but the offer itself is discounted — an investor buying a project in Tulsa generally works from roughly 65% to 80% of after-repair value, minus the repair budget. You are trading price for certainty, and whether that trade is good depends entirely on the condition of your house.

The honest test is simple. If your house would show well after a weekend of cleaning, list it. The Tulsa market is paying near list price in three weeks, and an owner-occupant with a loan will pay retail for a house they can move into. Handing that premium to an investor to save 3% in commission is a bad trade.

If the house needs $30,000 of work you do not have, or it is vacant, or the roof is at the end of its life and every buyer's lender is going to flag it, the listing math stops working. You are not comparing full price against a discount at that point. You are comparing a discounted offer against a lower price after two failed inspections, plus four more months of carrying it.

How do you actually lower the cost of selling a Tulsa house?

Four levers, in rough order of how much they move the number.

Price it right the first time. This is worth more than every other line combined. A house priced correctly sells near list in three weeks; a house priced 8% high sits, takes two reductions, and closes below where it would have started — after you carried it for four months. Every week of overpricing is paid for out of the same pocket.

Negotiate commission against the actual job. Not "what's your lowest," but a real conversation about the work the property requires and what is included. Ask what happens to the buyer-side compensation if the buyer is unrepresented.

Spend prep money only where it converts. Paint, clean, declutter, fix the obvious safety items, light fixtures, yard. Stop there. Skip the kitchen remodel; price the dated kitchen instead.

Pick your closing date deliberately. Property tax proration and one extra month of carrying cost are both decided by that date, and it is negotiable more often than sellers realize.

One thing I would not do is skip representation to save commission on a house that needs the help. For-sale-by-owner works on a clean, easy property with a motivated buyer already in hand. On a house with condition issues, an unusual title history or a tricky buyer, the savings evaporate into one bad negotiation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to sell a house in Tulsa?

Most Tulsa sellers pay between 7% and 10% of the sale price, or roughly $18,000 to $26,000 on a $260,000 house. Commission is the largest piece at 2.5% to 3% for the listing side plus whatever is agreed for the buyer's agent. Closing costs add another 1% to 2%, and prep, inspection credits and carrying costs make up the rest.

Who pays closing costs in Oklahoma, the buyer or the seller?

Both do, and the contract decides the split. Sellers customarily cover the documentary stamp tax, the abstract update, prorated property taxes and the release of their mortgage, while buyers cover lender fees, their own title and recording charges. The closing company's fee is commonly split, and several items, including who pays for the owner's title policy, are negotiable in Tulsa.

How much is the documentary stamp tax in Oklahoma?

Oklahoma charges $0.75 per $500 of consideration, or any fraction of it, on deeds where the value conveyed exceeds $100 under 68 O.S. § 3201. That works out to 0.15% of the sale price, or $390 on a $260,000 house. It is customarily paid by the seller at closing.

Do sellers still have to pay the buyer's agent in Oklahoma?

No. Since the 2024 NAR settlement, buyer's agent compensation is negotiated separately rather than posted with the listing, and a seller can decline to offer it. In practice most buyers in the $150,000 to $350,000 Tulsa range cannot pay their agent out of pocket, so declining usually comes back as a concession request instead of a saving.

What is an abstract of title and do I have to pay for it?

An abstract of title is the complete recorded history of a property, and Oklahoma is one of the few states that still relies on them. In a sale the abstract is brought current by a licensed abstractor before the buyer's attorney or title company examines it, and that update has customarily been the seller's expense. Cost depends on how much history has accumulated, from a few hundred dollars to well over a thousand on an old or divided parcel.

Is it cheaper to sell a house as-is in Tulsa?

The costs are lower but the net is usually lower too. A direct as-is sale removes commission, prep, inspection credits and most carrying costs, while the offer itself is discounted — investors in Tulsa generally work from roughly 65% to 80% of after-repair value minus repairs. It comes out ahead when condition, access or timing would keep the house from selling well on the open market.
