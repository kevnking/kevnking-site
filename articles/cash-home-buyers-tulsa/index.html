Cash home buyers in Tulsa: what they actually pay

How the number is built, what the law now requires of the person offering it, and when listing nets you more.

Cash home buyers in Tulsa typically pay 65% to 80% of a house's after-repair value, minus the cost of the repairs. On a house that would be worth $250,000 fixed up and needs $40,000 of work, that puts most real offers between $122,000 and $160,000. The discount isn't a trick — it's the price of closing in two weeks with no financing, no inspection repairs, no showings and no commission. Whether that trade is worth taking depends on two things you can measure this week: how far the house is from financeable, and what the waiting is costing you.

Almost nobody calls a cash buyer because they want less money. They call because something else is more expensive than the discount: a house they can't get financed, a deadline that doesn't move, four hundred miles between them and the property, or a rehab budget they don't have.

That's a legitimate trade. What isn't legitimate is not knowing how the number was built. So here is the arithmetic, from someone who writes it.

How much do cash home buyers in Tulsa actually pay?

Between 65% and 80% of after-repair value, minus repairs. Where you land inside that range is mostly about resale risk, not negotiation.

You get pushed toward the top of the range when the work is cosmetic and predictable — paint, flooring, a kitchen, a bath — and the house sits somewhere with liquid resale: Broken Arrow, Owasso, Bixby, Jenks, midtown Tulsa. Clean title and a vacant house help too, because both remove days and unknowns.

You get pushed toward the bottom when the problems are structural or mechanical, when the house is occupied, when the resale comps are thin or wildly inconsistent street to street, or when the scope is a full gut. A foundation problem moves a number further than almost anything else, because the repair bid has a wide range and the resale carries a permanent disclosure.

One more thing that moves the number, and most sellers never find out about it: whether the person making the offer is actually the buyer. A wholesaler has to leave room for the investor they plan to sell your contract to, so their offer is structurally lower than that end buyer's would have been. More on how to tell the difference below.

How is a cash offer on a Tulsa house calculated?

Start with after-repair value, subtract the repair budget, subtract the buyer's own resale costs, subtract holding cost, subtract the margin they need to do the deal. What's left is your offer.

Here's a real shape of a deal, using a three-bedroom, 1,400-square-foot house in east Tulsa:

  • After-repair value: $225,000. What it sells for finished, based on closed comps within a few blocks — not on a Zestimate.
  • Repairs: $45,000. Roof, HVAC, kitchen, one bathroom, flooring, paint, a few plumbing surprises.
  • Resale costs: about $15,700. Roughly 7% of the resale price — commission, closing costs, concessions to the eventual buyer.
  • Holding cost: about $6,000. Five months of taxes, insurance, utilities and money cost.
  • Required margin: $30,000. Less than that and the deal doesn't survive one bad surprise.

$225,000 minus $45,000 minus $15,700 minus $6,000 minus $30,000 leaves about $128,000. That's the offer.

You'll see the "70% rule" quoted everywhere — after-repair value times 0.70, minus repairs. On this house that shortcut produces $112,500, about $15,000 less than the built-up number. That's worth knowing, because the rule of thumb is a screening tool for investors looking at fifty houses, not a valuation of yours. Ask for the buildup, not the rule. A buyer who can't show you the four lines above either hasn't done the work or doesn't want you checking it.

The repair line is where the honest disagreements live. My number and your number will differ, and that's fine — but it should be a scope you can read, not a lump sum. You can run the same math yourself on my free deal analyzer before anyone quotes you anything.

Are "we buy houses" companies in Tulsa legitimate?

Some are funded buyers who close what they sign. Many are wholesalers who put your house under contract and then sell that contract to someone else. Both are legal in Oklahoma — but since November 1, 2025, the wholesalers owe you disclosures they didn't owe before, and you have a cancellation right you probably don't know about.

Senate Bill 1075 became law in 2025 and added 59 O.S. § 858-314 to the Oklahoma Real Estate License Code. If someone is wholesaling your house, the statute requires them to:

  • Disclose in writing, before you sign anything, that they intend to assign or resell their interest in your house at a higher price than they're offering you.
  • Give you the right to cancel the contract without penalty within two business days of signing it.
  • Hold any earnest money in an escrow account at a federally insured institution in Oklahoma — not in their own account.
  • Keep off your title. They're prohibited from placing a lien or otherwise clouding the property, which is what a recorded "notice of contract" does.

They also can't present themselves as your agent or imply a license they don't hold. Contracts missing the required disclosures are unenforceable by the wholesaler, and unlicensed activity carries real penalties.

So the vetting is short. Ask five questions and write the answers down:

  • Are you the buyer, or are you assigning this contract to someone else?
  • Can I see proof of funds — an account statement or a lender letter, not a screenshot?
  • Where is the earnest money held, and how much is it?
  • Is this price final, or does it get revised after a walkthrough? (Online "instant offers" are almost always the second one.)
  • Are you licensed in Oklahoma? You can check any license free at the Oklahoma Real Estate Commission.

A real buyer answers all five in one text message. Hesitation on any of them is the answer.

How fast can a cash sale on a Tulsa house actually close?

Seven to 21 days is realistic, and the binding constraint is title work, not money. Oklahoma doesn't run on a title commitment alone — most closings here still turn on an abstract of title and an attorney's written opinion on it.

For an ordinary Tulsa County parcel, an abstract update usually takes about two to seven business days, and the attorney's opinion another three to seven after that. Complicated history — unreleased mortgages, severed mineral interests, a probate in the chain — runs longer. Then anything the opinion flags has to be cured before you can deliver marketable title, and curative work ranges from an afternoon of paperwork to a court filing.

Which means: a buyer promising to close in three days is either carrying title risk they haven't explained to you, or telling you what you want to hear. Fourteen days on a clean parcel is fast and real. If you're up against a hard date — a sheriff's sale or a probate deadline — say so on the first call, because the title order should go out that day, not after inspection.

Cash offer or list it: what does each one actually net in Tulsa?

Listing wins on gross price almost every time. It's the costs, the calendar and the condition that decide which one wins on what reaches your account.

The Tulsa market is functioning, which matters here. Homes sold at a median of $259,828 in August 2026, up 3.9% year over year, at 97.9% of list price with a median 23 days on market, and 26.8% went above list, according to Redfin. A market-ready house in this town gets full retail from an owner-occupant with a loan. Don't hand that away out of exhaustion.

Run the same $250,000-after-repair, $40,000-of-work house three ways:

  • Fix it, then list it. Sells near $250,000. Subtract $40,000 of repairs, roughly $20,000 in selling costs at 8%, and about four months of carry. You net somewhere near $185,000 — and you funded the rehab and took the risk.
  • List it as-is. Buyers price condition harshly and financing gets picky, so call it $205,000. Subtract about $16,400 in costs and a $5,000 repair concession after inspection, and you're near $184,000 in 60 to 90 days, with showings.
  • Sell it as-is for cash. Around $145,000 in two weeks, no commission, no closing costs, no repairs, no showings.

That's roughly a $40,000 gap, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. Anyone who tells you a cash offer is "about the same as listing" is selling you something. The gap is the honest cost of speed and certainty. The only real question is whether you're actually in a position to capture the other $40,000 — which needs capital, access, time and tolerance for a deal falling apart on inspection.

When should you turn a cash offer down?

When the house would pass a lender's appraisal and you aren't under a clock. That's the short test, and it rules out more sellers than the cash-buyer ads suggest.

If the roof has life left, the systems work, and nothing about the house would scare an FHA appraiser, you are the retail market's customer, not mine. Twenty-three days on market is not a long time to wait for $40,000. Turn it down and list it.

Turn it down, too, when the deadline pressuring you turns out to be soft. Families in probate often think they're on a clock they're not actually on — I walked through the real Oklahoma timeline in selling an inherited house in Tulsa. And turn it down when the offer keeps changing: a price that drops after the walkthrough, then again a week before closing, is a negotiating strategy, not a valuation.

What I'd actually tell you to do is get both numbers from someone willing to say the listing number out loud. If the only number a buyer will give you is theirs, you're not being advised. You're being bid on.

What should you do before you sign anything?

In order, and most of it is free:

  • Get a real market analysis on closed comps — both the as-is number and the fixed-up number.
  • Get the repair scope in writing, line by line. A lump sum isn't a scope.
  • Ask for proof of funds before you talk price seriously.
  • Read the contract for the assignment disclosure. If they intend to resell your contract, the statute says they have to tell you in writing first.
  • Don't sign the same day you're pitched. You have two business days to cancel a wholesaler's contract, but zero reason to need them.
  • Confirm who's paying closing costs, and get the closing date in the contract rather than "as soon as possible."

Frequently Asked Questions

How much do cash home buyers pay for a house in Tulsa?

Most cash buyers in Tulsa pay 65% to 80% of a house's after-repair value, minus the cost of the repairs. On a house worth $250,000 fixed up that needs $40,000 of work, that means roughly $122,000 to $160,000. Houses needing only cosmetic work in strong resale areas land near the top of that range, and structural or occupied properties near the bottom.

Is the 70% rule how cash offers are really calculated?

The 70% rule — after-repair value times 0.70, minus repairs — is a screening shortcut investors use to sort through many houses quickly, not a valuation of a specific one. A proper offer is built from after-repair value minus repairs, resale costs, holding costs and the buyer's required margin. On most houses that buildup comes out somewhat higher than the flat 70% rule, so ask for the buildup.

Are "we buy houses" companies legal in Oklahoma?

Yes, both funded cash buyers and wholesalers operate legally in Oklahoma, but wholesalers have been regulated since Senate Bill 1075 took effect November 1, 2025. Under 59 O.S. § 858-314 they must disclose in writing before you sign that they intend to resell their interest at a higher price, hold earnest money in an in-state escrow account, and stay off your title. A contract missing the required disclosures is unenforceable by the wholesaler.

Can you cancel a contract with a cash home buyer in Oklahoma?

If the buyer is a wholesaler, Oklahoma law gives you the right to cancel the contract without penalty within two business days of signing it. That right does not automatically apply to a funded buyer purchasing the house for their own account, so read the contract's own termination terms. Either way, the better move is not signing on the day you are pitched.

How fast can you close a cash sale on a Tulsa house?

Seven to 21 days is realistic for a clean parcel. The limit is Oklahoma's title process rather than the money: an abstract update on an ordinary Tulsa County property runs about two to seven business days, and the attorney's title opinion another three to seven after that. Anything the opinion flags has to be cured before closing, which can add days or weeks.

Do you pay commission or closing costs on a cash sale?

In a direct as-is sale there is no listing commission, and most cash buyers pay the standard closing costs themselves, so the offer price is close to what you actually receive. Existing mortgages, property tax prorations and any liens still come out of the proceeds at closing. Always get in writing which costs the buyer is covering before you sign.

The short version

A cash offer in Tulsa is 65% to 80% of after-repair value minus repairs, and it is built from four lines you are entitled to see. Ask whether the person offering is the buyer or an assignor, because Oklahoma now requires that answer in writing and gives you two business days to walk. Expect 7 to 21 days to close, governed by the abstract and the attorney's opinion. And if the house is financeable and nothing is forcing your hand, list it — the market here sells in 23 days at nearly full list, and that gap is worth more than the convenience.
